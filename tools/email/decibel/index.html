This is the largest single week change at Decibel since we started reporting on it, so I want to be direct about what moved and about what it does to last week's note.

The offer went from six weeks free to two on 9/11. In the same seven days the one bedroom asking rent came down 9.6% to $1,856 while the market held at $2,072, and the one bedroom concession went from 9.5% to 0.5%. Run those two together and the net effect on a one bedroom is about $32 a month. The money moved from the concession line to the headline ask rather than leaving the building. At two weeks you are now tied with two comps for the shallowest free rent offer among the fifteen properties running one, and you hold about $43,000 a year more than the field gives away, scoped to the 59 homes that actually turn at your 30.6% blended renewal rate rather than to all 85.

Last week's recommendation is retired, and the report says so on its own face. That note said hold at six weeks until exposure reached the trigger. There are no six weeks left to hold, so the recommendation has been overtaken rather than reversed. The replacement is to hold the two weeks for two full weeks and judge them on exposure.

The pricing argument we have made for six editions is now closed. Your effective rent is $2.85 per square foot against a set average of $2.88, a gap of 1.2%, where last week it was $2.65 against $2.85 and a gap of 6.9%. On what you advertise today the same asking rent nets $2.93 per foot, which is above the set. The dollar figure still looks wide, $1,798 against $1,912, but your listed homes average 632 square feet against the set's 664, and size accounts for about four fifths of what is left. Decibel is no longer the cheap building per foot, and anyone still saying it is, is reading the dollar column.

The one line that got worse is exposure. 12.9% and eleven homes advertised, up from 10.6% and nine, second highest of the twenty one properties tracked against a 6.3% peer average. Occupancy followed it to 91.8%. Four of those eleven homes are rent capped, so no offer reaches them; on your market rate book alone exposure is 9.6%, and that is the more useful number when someone asks.

What I will not tell you is that the cut caused anything, or that it is working. You signed six leases in thirty days against a set average of 5.0, so you are still above the field on the month. Your last two weeks are blank. The first of those blanks closed on 9/6 and the offer changed on 9/11, so the slowdown started before the offer moved, and two days is not a window. Across all twenty one properties, offer depth does not sort absorption: the deepest offer in the field and no offer at all both signed nine leases, and the two fullest buildings run no offer. That is a reason to hold rather than a promise that two weeks will fill the building. The measurement that settles it is exposure on 9/26.

Two things that cost no rent. You refresh pricing every 25 days while thirteen of the twenty comps refresh inside sixteen, and you have just made a large price change you now cannot observe for three weeks. Moving to weekly is free. And your published review rating is 1.1 against a field running 2.7 to 5.0, with two properties inside 0.6 miles sitting above 4.5. That is the widest gap to the field anywhere in this report and the only one you can close without giving up a dollar.

One correction to record. Last week we scoped the annual figures to 60 homes at a 29.4% renewal rate. Rebuilding that from the plan level book gives 59 homes at 30.6%, and the corrected figure is used in every annualised number this week.

Our data resolves your building to 85 homes. If that does not match your own count, tell us the number you carry and we will reconcile every figure to it rather than defend ours.
