AI can describe your photos. What would it say about yours?

Image tools can look at a photo and label what's in it: a pool, a dog park, a tree lined street, a home office nook. Google already uses this kind of image understanding in its search tools, and AI tools increasingly work with what they see as well as what they read.

That changes what a good photo library looks like. Stock shots of strangers and dark, outdated amenity photos give these tools very little that's true about your community. Real, current, well lit photos of the spaces renters care about give them accurate details to work with.

Try this: open your Google profile and your website gallery side by side. If someone only had these photos, what would they think your community offers? Is anything you're proud of missing?

What's the one amenity your photos never seem to capture well?

Would AI pick you from your bio?

"Your trusted real estate partner, serving the greater metro area" reads fine to a person skimming. To an AI tool trying to match a buyer's question to an agent, it gives very little to match.

When someone asks for an agent who knows mid century homes in Willo, or who handles corporate relocations, AI tools tend to favor pages that say exactly that. Who you help, where you work and what you're known for, written in specific terms.

Take ten minutes with your bio this week:
Name the neighborhoods you actually work in.
Name the buyers or sellers you serve best.
Add the details only you can claim, like years in a specific area or a type of home you know well.

If a buyer described their perfect agent to an AI tool, would your bio match?

Happy Halloween. Here are the scariest things AI might be repeating about your community.

1. A special that ended months ago, still sitting on a listing site somewhere.
2. An amenity that closed for renovation, still listed as open.
3. A tough review with no reply, so only one side of the story is out there.

None of these come from bad intentions. They pile up quietly as teams get busy. And AI tools can pull from any of these places, and a renter can't tell which detail is out of date.

The fix is simple: a short checklist, run on a schedule, by someone who owns it.
